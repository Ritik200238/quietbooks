01

A public ledger is a bad place to keep your commercial terms

Every invoice settled on a transparent chain publishes what a business spends the most effort keeping quiet.

  • Your prices. A competitor reads what you charge each customer and undercuts by a margin they can calculate exactly.
  • Your customers. Counterparty addresses are a client list anyone can scrape.
  • Your position. Invoice volume and payment timing expose revenue and cashflow between funding rounds.

Hiding the data is easy: keep it off the chain. The hard part is proving, later, that what you are showing an auditor is the same truth you committed to at the time, without having published it in the meantime.

02

What is actually on the ledger

Public
  • That the invoice exists
  • Both parties, as rotatable pseudonyms
  • Issue date, due date, status
  • Four 32-byte digests
  • Aggregate counts across the deployment
Never written by any circuit
  • The amount
  • The tax
  • The currency
  • Line items and memo
  • Order reference
  • Every commitment opening

A test asserts this rather than asserting it in prose: the serialised anchor is searched for the invoice amount, and the test fails if it appears. The end-to-end run repeats the same search against state read back from the indexer.

03

The finding that shaped the product

We set out to have the contract hold the money and hide the amount. It cannot, and finding out why produced a better design.

Why custody publishes the amount

receiveShielded requires its coin argument to be disclosed, and a ShieldedCoinInfo carries a plaintext value. OpenZeppelin's own ShieldedTreasury states it plainly in its header: “this treasury’s holdings are public.”

There is also no standard-library circuit that derives a Zswap note commitment from coin data, so a contract cannot re-derive one in-circuit and bind it to a private amount.

What works instead

claimZswapCoinReceive(note) makes the ledger refuse a transaction unless a specific Zswap output is present in it, using only a 32-byte commitment. That commitment reveals neither value nor recipient.

So settlement happens peer to peer, where Zswap hides the amount, and the contract binds that transfer to the invoice atomically.

The chain learns that an invoice was settled by a real shielded output. It never learns for how much. An auditor holding the opening can verify the amount exactly.

04

Three ways to settle, and what each one costs you

Path Caller Amount on chain What binds it
settleWithNote Buyer hidden The ledger refuses the call unless the named Zswap output exists in the same transaction
settleAttested Seller hidden The seller vouches for receipt. Seller-only by design: they are the party who loses by lying
fundEscrow → releaseEscrow Buyer public The contract holds the coin, releases on confirmation, refunds after a deadline, or obeys an arbiter
Escrow is offered because locked funds are sometimes worth more than a hidden figure. The interface says so before you use it, rather than in a footnote.
05

Selective audit, checked eight ways

The seller grants one auditor specific fields of one invoice until a chosen date. On chain: the hash of an audit key, the field set, and an expiry. The key and the data travel out of band in an encrypted envelope.

The nine disclosable fields

amount · tax · dueDate · buyer · seller · currency · items · memo · orderRef

Compact has no bitwise operators, so a grant is a nine-slot boolean vector in-circuit. The envelope still carries the integer mask auditors expect.

What the validator proves

Version, revocation, expiry, key-hash match, containment, decryption integrity, per-field commitment recomputation, and that all nine fold back into the field root the chain has held since issuance.

The check that matters most is containment: an envelope disclosing more than its grant allows must fail, not be trusted. It is enforced against the fields inside the ciphertext, not the header, because whoever seals the envelope holds the key and can seal a header that lies.

06

A payment record the party cannot write themselves

The contract keeps counts, and only counts, against each party key: settled, settled on time, cancelled, disputes opened, disputes lost. No amounts, and no link from a count back to an individual invoice.

  • Written by the contract, not by the party it describes. That is the whole difference between this and a claim on a website.
  • Public by construction. They are counts in public ledger state, so a prospective customer can read them for any party key without asking permission.
  • The threshold proof over them is Wave 2. “At least twenty settled, at least eighteen on time” as one boolean, disclosing none of the counts, is written and tested but not in this deploy. The next slide says why.

The honest cost, stated in the contract header and asserted in a test: counters are keyed by party key, so rotating a PIN for unlinkability also resets the visible history.

07

Why twelve entry points, and how we knew

A deploy carries one verifier key per entry point and has to fit inside one block. Ours did not. The node's entire answer was:

1010: Invalid Transaction: Transaction would exhaust the block limits

It names neither the limit nor the margin, and the wallet's Effect runtime buried even that behind a generic submission error. So we measured instead of guessing: a generator builds a contract with N entry points, compiles it, and deploys it.

QuietBooks, 18 entry points44,96489.9%refused
 Persistent writes, in bytes, against the documented 50,000-byte block limit. One transaction gets roughly two thirds of a block, not all of it.

Four of the six circuits removed were entry points nothing called, including a duplicate of a rule the off-chain validator already enforced. Two were real features deferred to Wave 2. Splitting escrow into a second contract would have kept everything and was rejected: Midnight has no cross-contract calls, so releasing an escrow and marking its invoice settled would stop being atomic.

Losing atomicity over money is a worse trade than deferring a proof. The measurement, the options and the reasoning are all in DECISIONS.md.

08

Identity without msg.sender

How a party proves who they are

partyKey = persistentHash([tag, instanceSalt, pin, secret])

A 32-byte secret held in the wallet's private state. Changing the PIN yields an unrelated key and breaks linkability with the old one.

What is deliberately never used

ownPublicKey() appears nowhere in an authorisation path. It returns a prover-claimed value with no cryptographic binding to the transaction signer, so any assertion resting on it is bypassable.

instanceSalt is sealed at deployment, so one wallet secret yields different identities on different deployments and cannot be correlated across them.

A bug the tests nearly missed

An integer encoder wrote big-endian where the circuit reads little-endian, so amount commitments never appeared in the field root. Every audit envelope built on them would have been unverifiable.

Nothing caught it because the test compared the helper against a function that called the helper. Both sides moved together and the assertion held.

What replaced it

The test now folds our own field commitments independently and compares the result to the circuit's own root. The only shared input is the invoice frame, so a one-byte disagreement cannot pass.

Reintroducing the old encoding fails seven tests. That was checked by doing it.

10

What is not built

A roadmap that lists only wins is not a roadmap.

  • Wallet-side note binding. The contract half of settleWithNote is implemented and tested. Wiring it to the wallet's coin-commitment tracking, so the payment and the contract call ride in one transaction, is not finished; the end-to-end run settles by attestation, and escrow is the path that moves real shielded value today.
  • The reliability threshold proof. Written and tested, deferred to Wave 2 for the block-budget reason above. The counters it proves over are already being kept.
  • Administrator rotation. Removed for the same reason. The administrator is fixed at deploy; the emergency stop still works.
  • Local proof server. Every user needs one running. True of every Midnight DApp today; in-browser proving is the fix when it reaches the wallet.
  • Partial payments. Every invoice settles once, in full. No recurring schedules, no multi-currency netting.

12

Who this is for

Today

Teams already invoicing in stablecoins who do not want their price list on a public explorer, and who still need an accountant, a tax authority or an investor to be able to verify a specific figure on demand.

The wedge

Grant programmes and foundations that disburse on milestones and publish aggregates. They need the totals public and the individual awards private, which is exactly the split this contract enforces.

Selective disclosure is the product, not a feature of it. Nobody adopts privacy that stops them proving things when asked.
